# Research sweep: completeness critique

> **Pre-verification research (2026-10-16).** Where this conflicts with [`docs/research/fact-sheet.md`](../fact-sheet.md), the fact sheet wins. Content about a specific third-party product was removed under the docs policy ([ADR-034](../../adr/ADR-034-docs-policy.md)).

## Coverage assessment

Overall the sweep is strong and mostly built on primary sources. Confidence is medium-high on the strategic picture, medium on exact broker behaviour, and low-medium on three areas: managed RabbitMQ services, the 2026 web/team/AI competitors, and enterprise sign-in and network access.

SOLID
   - On its own site: all 63 sitemap pages (including which docs are empty stubs), the changelog, the public update files (which reveal builds 1.0.0 and 1.1.0 that the changelog leaves out), the 10-item roadmap, the license-manager code, and the terms and privacy pages.
   - Features, release pace (1.9.0 on 2026-08-18, nothing since, no roadmap item shipped), the Electron stack, the direct HTTP-plus-AMQP design with no relay server, and the licensing friction are all well evidenced.
   - The app itself was never run.
2. **The official management API and UI** analysis is backed by source code:
   - an inventory of about 130 HTTP routes;
   - the operations that exist only in the command-line tools;
   - what breaks, by version, when statistics are switched off;
   - CORS, OAuth and hardening settings, and the features reserved for the paid Tanzu edition;
   - the core team's 2026-09-23 statement on modernising the UI.
3. **RabbitMQ 4.x behaviour** was read at source level (rabbit_fifo, rabbit_channel, rabbit_mgmt_wm_queue_get). It produced the key insight:
   - every "peek" at a classic or quorum queue has side effects, and the rules change between versions;
   - live taps have blind spots;
   - the broker no longer interprets an x-death header that a client publishes.
4. **The broader work agrees on the big conclusions.** This covers the landscape of about 40 tools, patterns from other brokers, user pain mined from Stack Overflow and GitHub, and the architecture options (dual-mode precedents, client-library maturity per language, desktop shells, code signing):
   - there is no de facto open-source RabbitMQ UI;
   - a web edition needs a server-side engine;
   - one engine and one UI shipped as both desktop and self-hosted web is the winning pattern;
   - the business model should be open core (single-user free, governance paid);
   - a stream browser and a linter are features only paid Tanzu offers today.

THIN OR MISSING
- **No lab testing anywhere.** Every safety-critical behaviour comes from reading source code or from a single source. The reports disagree on details, and the claim that peeking a dead-letter queue can drop messages comes from an author who appears linked to the competing Warren product.
- **Managed brokers** (Amazon MQ, CloudAMQP, LavinMQ, Tanzu, Kubernetes operator and Helm deployments) were flagged as open questions by four angles. My quick check found that Amazon MQ 4.2 brokers create quorum queues by default and block upgrades from 3.x to 4.x while classic queues exist. Both affect how temporary queues and taps work, and whether the tool can avoid broker plugins.
- **Enterprise sign-in and network access** are only sketched as ideas:
  - passing a user's OAuth2 token through to the broker;
  - how a desktop app signs in as a public client;
  - refreshing tokens on long-lived AMQP connections;
  - mTLS and LDAP;
  - SSH, proxy and Kubernetes access paths;
  - stream connections being redirected to advertised hosts;
  - the team server's threat model, including server-side request forgery (SSRF).
- **The 2026 web/team/AI competitors** are known mostly from Warren's own comparison blog, and none of them appear in the landscape matrix.
  - QueueLens and HZN Queue Console have no findable URLs in quick searches.
  - Qarote exists but only does monitoring; SSO/SAML, role-based access, audit and alerts sit in a $99/month cloud tier capped at 100 queues per broker.
- **Framework failure formats are not catalogued**, although the top-ranked dead-letter feature depends on them. Missing frameworks include MassTransit (and its v9 commercial licence), NServiceBus, Spring, Celery and its Flower UI, Symfony and Laravel.

Also thin:
- performance of the management API and Prometheus at 10,000–100,000 queues, and how a team server can poll once for many users;
- market size and willingness to pay specific to RabbitMQ;
- evidence from Reddit, IDE marketplaces, the iOS App Store, X and YouTube;
- the MQTT/STOMP user persona;
- accessibility and translation;
- team-server high availability and durability of long-running jobs.

Two questions are for the user, not for research:
- **Who is building this** (team size and language skills). This drives the Go vs TypeScript vs .NET choice, on which the reports disagree.
- **What "web application" means:** self-hosted only, or also a vendor-hosted service. A hosted service means holding customers' broker credentials, SOC 2, and agents to reach private networks.

Most angles ran out of the shared web-search budget, so many 2026-dated facts rest on a single direct page fetch.

## Gaps (each became a verification or gap-research task)

### Gap 1: No lab testing of message-safety behaviour across RabbitMQ 3.13–4.4 (peek, dead-letter inspection, taps, firehose, x-death)

**Why it matters.** Safe peek, dead-letter inspection and replay, and live spy are the product's headline features core. All the evidence comes from reading source code or from single sources. The reports disagree on whether nack counts toward the limit on 4.0–4.2, whether consumer timeouts count, whether exchange-to-exchange taps are side-effect-free, and how far a firehose switched on over HTTP reaches. The dead-letter carry-over-drop claim comes from an author who appears linked to a competitor. A wrong assumption silently drops, dead-letters or diverts production messages. Versions 3.13, 4.1 and 4.2 keep commercial support until 2027–2030, so all three behaviour eras must be handled correctly.

*Its headline features are non-destructive 'peek' of queue messages, dead-letter-queue (DLQ) inspection and replay, a live 'spy'/wire-tap, and a stream browser. Desk research as of 2026-10-16 produced these unverified claims, which you must test. (C1) Quorum queues (QQ) default to delivery-limit 20 since RabbitMQ 4.0. On 4.0–4.2 the limit is reportedly checked against an internal acquired_count that increments on EVERY return, including basic.nack(requeue=true), so nack-based peeks consume the budget. This comes from reading deps/rabbit/src/rabbit_fifo.erl at v4.0.1 and v4.2.0. (C2) From 4.3, per the table at https://www.rabbitmq.com/docs/quorum-queues: basic.nack, AMQP 1.0 released, modified(delivery-failed=false), intra-cluster partition and consumer timeout increment only x-acquired-count. basic.reject, AMQP 1.0 rejected, modified(delivery-failed=true) and client crash/connection loss also increment x-delivery-count, which is checked against the limit. One report instead says consumer timeout counts. Unknown: graceful channel.close, connection.close or basic.cancel while holding unacked deliveries. (C3) A post by an author apparently linked to the competing Warren DLQ product (https://dev.to/viktorbaumann/your-quorum-dead-letter-queue-can-drop-the-dead-letters-x-delivery-count-travels-along-240n) claims that a message dead-lettered for delivery_limit arrives in a quorum DLQ with its count already above the limit. It is then dropped on its next return, even a nack peek, unless the DLQ has its own DLX or delivery-limit -1. (C4) The management API call POST /api/queues/{vhost}/{q}/get runs count×basic.get, then settles: ack_requeue_true→basic.nack(requeue), reject_requeue_true→basic.reject(requeue). Large gets reportedly crashed a node (github.com/rabbitmq/rabbitmq-server/discussions/13060). Bound to x-consistent-hash, x-modulus-hash or x-local-random exchanges, it may steal messages from the real queues. Any tap may turn unroutable messages into routed ones, so alternate exchanges and mandatory basic.return stop firing. Another report claims exchange-to-exchange taps (rabtap) leave delivery untouched. (C6) Enabling the firehose over HTTP (PUT /api/vhosts/{v} with tracing true) may start tracing only on the node serving the request, and is not persisted across restarts. (C7) Since 4.0 a client-published x-death header is not interpreted and is replaced on the next dead-lettering. (C8) 4.3 denies transient non-exclusive queues and global QoS by default, requires configure permission for passive declares, and no longer applies consumer timeouts to classic queues. METHOD: If Docker is available, run official images rabbitmq:3.13.7-management, 4.0.9, 4.1.8, 4.2.11 and 4.3.6, plus a 4.4/5.0 alpha from https://github.com/rabbitmq/server-packages/releases if one exists. Use single nodes, and a 3-node 4.3 cluster for the firehose and partition tests. Clients: Go amqp091-go or Python pika for AMQP 0-9-1, rabbitmq-amqp-go-client or the Python AMQP 1.0 client, and curl for HTTP. For every version, repeat each settle path 25 times on one QQ message, with and without a DLX: get+nack, get+reject, consume(prefetch)+nack, consume+reject, AMQP 1.0 released, modified with df=false and df=true, graceful channel close while holding unacked messages, graceful connection close, TCP kill, cancel then close, QQ consumer timeout (4.3), and HTTP /get with ack_requeue_true and reject_requeue_true. Record x-delivery-count, x-acquired-count, the redelivered flag, the queue position, and the iteration at which the message is dead-lettered (with x-death reason) or dropped. Also run these tests: (1) C3, using DLQs that are a QQ with the default limit, a QQ with limit -1, a QQ with its own DLX, and a classic queue. (2) Whether 4.3 delayed retry (x-delayed-retry-type returned/all) delays a message after one nack peek. (3) Classic-queue requeue position and hold behaviour. (4) Single-active-consumer queues: does basic.get bypass the active consumer, and can a browsing consumer become the active one? (5) An exclusive queue declared with no x-queue-type in a vhost whose default_queue_type is quorum (Amazon MQ 4.2 defaults to quorum). (6) Taps on direct, topic, headers (with and without binding arguments), consistent-hash, modulus-hash, local-random and E2E bindings, including alternate-exchange and mandatory outcomes. (7) Which cluster nodes emit amq.rabbitmq.trace after the HTTP firehose toggle, and whether it survives a restart. (8) x-death after republishing and re-dead-lettering over 0-9-1, and x-opt-deaths over AMQP 1.0. (9) Stream reads via 0-9-1 x-stream-offset and via AMQP 1.0 property filters (4.1) and SQL filters (4.2), and what HTTP /get returns for a stream. If Docker is unavailable, verify each item from source at the exact tags (rabbit_fifo.erl return_one/incr_msg, rabbit_fifo_client.erl, rabbit_channel.erl, rabbit_amqp_session.erl, rabbit_mgmt_wm_queue_get.erl, rabbit_trace.erl, rabbit_vhost.erl, mc_amqpl.erl) and from GitHub issues. Optional: measure GET /api/queues latency, payload size and broker CPU at 10k and 50k queues with page_size=500, columns=, disable_stats=true and enable_queue_totals=true.

### Gap 3: Enterprise sign-in and network access only sketched (OAuth2/OIDC pass-through, mTLS/LDAP, tunnels and proxies, stream advertised hosts, team-server threat model)

**Why it matters.** The web edition's core design choice is between two models. In one, each user's identity-provider token is passed through to RabbitMQ, so the broker enforces that user's permissions and audit entries name the user. In the other, the server holds service credentials and enforces its own role-based access. Tunnels, proxies and the stream protocol's advertised-host redirection also decide whether the stream browser and AMQP features work at all from developer laptops.

### Gap 5: Client-framework failure formats and ecosystems not catalogued (MassTransit, NServiceBus, Spring, Celery, Symfony, Laravel, NestJS…)

**Why it matters.** The top-ranked differentiator is a dead-letter operations centre that groups failures by exception, decodes framework headers and replays messages correctly. It cannot be designed without the exact header names, queue naming, message envelopes and replay rules of the frameworks most RabbitMQ users run. Framework communities are also the main distribution channel to those users. Ecosystem shifts that may create migration demand are missing from the reports, such as MassTransit v9's commercial licence and the archived delayed-message plugin.

**Research task.** CONTEXT: The top-ranked differentiator for our planned RabbitMQ desktop + self-hosted web tool is a dead-letter operations centre. It groups failed messages by exception fingerprint and replays them to their original destination, throttled, with publisher confirms, verification and undo. ## Contradictions

- **rabbitmq-4x-semantics vs user-pain-points (cross-broker-inspiration and rabbitmq-management-surface leave it unverified):** Quorum-queue counting on 4.0–4.2. rabbitmq-4x-semantics read rabbit_fifo.erl at v4.0.1 and v4.2.0. It says the limit is checked against acquired_count, which every return increments, basic.nack included, so nack-based peeks use up the 20-delivery budget. user-pain-points says, with no version qualifier, that 'basic.reject and client crash or connection loss increment delivery-count; basic.nack does not'. It recommends 'Peek with basic.get plus nack(requeue), never reject' as the safe design. cross-broker-inspiration and rabbitmq-management-surface treat the 4.0–4.2 behaviour as an unverified inference. Which one is right decides whether nack-based peeking is safe on the commercially supported 4.1 and 4.2.
- **rabbitmq-tools-landscape vs rabbitmq-4x-semantics (user-pain-points flags the ambiguity):** tools-landscape says that from 4.3 'basic.reject, connection loss and consumer timeout' count toward the delivery limit. The rabbitmq-4x-semantics table says a 4.3 consumer timeout increments only x-acquired-count and does not count. user-pain-points notes that two readings of the docs disagreed.
